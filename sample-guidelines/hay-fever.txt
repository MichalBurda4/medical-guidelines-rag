Hay Fever (Allergic Rhinitis) — Practical Guideline Notes

Side effects / safety
- Intranasal corticosteroids are first-line for moderate-severe symptoms. Common local side effects include nasal irritation, dryness, and epistaxis.
- Oral antihistamines may cause drowsiness (especially first-generation agents). Second-generation antihistamines have lower sedation risk.
- Avoid prolonged use of topical nasal decongestants due to rebound congestion (rhinitis medicamentosa).
- Eye drops for allergic conjunctivitis are usually well tolerated; report persistent eye pain or vision changes promptly.

Adherence
- Daily consistent use of intranasal steroids improves control; onset of benefit is usually within a few days, with fuller effect after 1–2 weeks.
- Patients should be shown correct spray technique; poor technique is a frequent cause of treatment failure.
- Adherence drops when symptoms improve seasonally; counseling to continue therapy during the pollen season is recommended.
- Simple reminders (phone alarms, linking spray use to brushing teeth) improve adherence in adolescents.

Age considerations
- Second-generation antihistamines are preferred in school-age children when sedation would impair learning.
- Intranasal corticosteroids approved for pediatric use should be chosen by age label; monitor growth with longer-term steroid exposure.
- Older adults may be more sensitive to anticholinergic and sedating effects of first-generation antihistamines.
- For teens driving or sitting exams, avoid first-generation antihistamines because of sleepiness and slower reaction time.

Pregnancy
- Intranasal corticosteroids with established pregnancy data (for example budesonide where locally approved) are generally preferred over oral agents when topical therapy is adequate.
- Avoid unnecessary systemic decongestants in pregnancy; discuss risks/benefits with the clinician.
- Non-drug measures (allergen avoidance, saline irrigation) are safe supportive options.

Symptom onset and effect
- Oral second-generation antihistamines often relieve sneezing and itching within 1–3 hours.
- Intranasal antihistamines can act within minutes for nasal itching and sneezing.
- Intranasal corticosteroids reduce congestion more effectively than antihistamines alone, but require regular use for best results.
- Combination therapy (intranasal steroid + antihistamine) may be considered when monotherapy is insufficient.

Business / service delivery notes
- Seasonal pollen peaks drive call-center and pharmacy demand; stock second-generation antihistamines and steroid sprays ahead of peak weeks.
- Patient education materials should cover spray technique and seasonal adherence to reduce unnecessary repeat visits.
- Track treatment failure linked to poor technique as a quality indicator in allergy pathways.
